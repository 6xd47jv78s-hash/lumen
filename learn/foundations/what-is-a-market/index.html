What a market actually is

Price discovery, order books, and why price is a record of agreement

Most people picture a market as a place where things have prices. That's backwards. A market is a machine for producing prices, and the price you see quoted is not a fact about the asset — it's a record of the most recent moment two people disagreed enough to trade with each other.

Understanding this properly changes how you read everything else. So let's look at what's actually underneath a price.

The order book

At any moment, an exchange holds a list of everyone's resting orders — who wants to buy, who wants to sell, at what price, and how much. That list is the order book, and it's the real object the market is made of.

The highest price a buyer will pay is the bid. The lowest price a seller will accept is the ask. The gap between them — three cents here — is the spread, and it is a real cost: buy at 184.25 and immediately sell at 184.22 and you have lost money without the price moving at all.

Notice what has to happen for a trade to occur. Someone has to cross the spread — a buyer has to accept 184.25 rather than waiting at 184.22. The instant they do, a trade prints, and that print is what gets reported as "the price."

●What a price actually means

The quoted price is the last agreement, not the current value. It tells you what one buyer and one seller settled on, for however much size they traded. It does not promise you can buy or sell at that price, and in a thin market you often can't.

Price discovery

Price discovery is the continuous process of that agreement being renegotiated. New information arrives — an earnings report, a rate decision, a rumour, someone's fund needing cash — and traders adjust the orders they're willing to leave resting. The book reshapes, the spread moves, and the printed price follows.

This is why the phrase "the market thinks" is a useful shorthand and a dangerous one. The market doesn't think. It aggregates. When a stock falls 8% on good news, nothing malfunctioned — it means the buyers who were going to buy had already bought, and the sellers outnumbered whoever was left.

Liquidity: the property that decides everything

Liquidity is how much size the book can absorb without the price moving much. It's the difference between a market where a $10,000 order does nothing and one where the same order moves price 4%.

Apple trades tens of millions of shares a day with a spread of a cent or two. A small-cap stock might trade 40,000 shares with a 2% spread. On a chart they look like the same kind of object. In practice they are completely different instruments — the second one will hand you slippage, violent wicks and false breakouts that the first one won't.

▮From the desk

Every strange thing a chart does has a plausible explanation in the order book. A price that stalls repeatedly at $50.00 isn't obeying a magic line — there is a large resting sell order at $50.00. When you learn support and resistance later in this course, this is the mechanism underneath it.

Who is on the other side?

Every trade has two sides, and someone is taking the opposite of your view. It's worth knowing who. Roughly, the participants are:

  • —[[Market maker|Market makers]] — firms quoting both a bid and an ask continuously, earning the spread. They aren't betting on direction; they're renting you immediacy.
  • —Institutions — pension funds, asset managers, hedge funds. They move size, which means they often have to work orders over hours or days rather than buying in one go.
  • —Retail traders — individuals. Collectively significant, individually invisible.
  • —Algorithms — the majority of order flow in major markets. They react in microseconds and never get bored.

You are not going to out-react an algorithm or out-research a fund with fifty analysts. What you can do is choose your timeframe, control your risk, and be patient in a way that professionals with quarterly performance reviews often can't afford to be. That's a real edge, and it's the one this course is built around.

◆Key takeaway

A price is the most recent agreement between a buyer and a seller — not a measure of value. It is produced by the order book, and how reliable it is depends on liquidity. Everything you'll learn about reading charts is ultimately a way of inferring what's happening in that book from the trail it leaves behind.
