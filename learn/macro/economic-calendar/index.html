Reading an economic calendar

CPI, NFP, FOMC — and why traders mostly use the calendar to stay out

An economic calendar lists upcoming data releases with the time, the previous value, and the consensus forecast. Every trading platform has one, and most beginners ignore it — which is how people end up holding a position through the most violent ten minutes of the month by accident.

The releases that matter

●Three numbers, one that matters

Every entry shows previous, consensus and, after release, actual. Price reacts to actual − consensus. Previous is context only.

Watch for revisions too. A strong jobs number alongside a large downward revision to last month's figure is often net negative, and the first automated reaction frequently gets it wrong for a minute or two.

What a release actually looks like

Three things happen in that window that should worry any trader holding a position through it:

  • —Spreads widen dramatically. Market makers pull quotes when they don't know where price is going. A spread that is normally one tick can become ten.
  • —Slippage is severe. A stop order triggered in that spike converts to a market order and fills wherever there's liquidity, which can be far from your trigger.
  • —Direction is close to random in the first seconds. The initial move is dominated by automated execution and triggered stops, and reversals within a minute are common.

How experienced traders use it

Almost entirely defensively. This surprises people who expect it to be a trading opportunity.

  1. 1

    Check it before every session

    Thirty seconds. What's out today, and at what time? This is the entire habit.

  2. 2

    Don't open new positions right before a high-impact release

    You're taking a coin flip with a widened spread attached, which is a negative-expectancy bet regardless of your analysis.

  3. 3

    Reduce or close existing positions

    Or accept the risk deliberately, with a size that assumes your stop fills badly. The mistake is holding through it without having decided to.

  4. 4

    Wait for the dust to settle

    Many traders don't act for 15–30 minutes after a major release. The move that holds is more informative than the move that spikes.

▮From the desk

There's a real disagreement here worth knowing about. Some traders specialise in news events, trading the volatility deliberately. It can be done — but it demands fast execution, wide stops sized for the spread, and acceptance of severe slippage. It's a specialist discipline, not a general-purpose technique, and it's a poor place for anyone to start.

The stock-specific calendar

Individual shares have their own scheduled event: earnings. The principle is identical but the risk is higher, because earnings land outside market hours and produce gaps your stop cannot protect you from.

The rule most swing traders follow is simple and absolute: know when every position you hold reports, and decide deliberately whether to be in it. Holding through earnings without meaning to is one of the most common unforced errors there is.

◆Key takeaway

The calendar shows previous, consensus and actual — only actual − consensus moves price. Scheduled releases produce widened spreads, severe slippage and near-random initial direction. Experienced traders use the calendar defensively: check it every session, avoid opening into high-impact events, and always know when your holdings report earnings.
